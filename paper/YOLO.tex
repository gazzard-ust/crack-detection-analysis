% ==============================================================
%   ABSTRACT
% ==============================================================
\begin{abstract}
Automated visual inspection of piping systems is essential for structural integrity monitoring, yet the effect of pipe surface properties on detection accuracy remains underexplored. This paper examines whether the visual complexity of the pipe surface degrades crack detection accuracy, even under class-balanced training conditions. Three crack types were created on surfaces ranging from smooth PVC to textured tissue paper, with 400 labels per class. Two object detection models, YOLO-World XL and YOLOv8x, were each trained five times with different random seeds on an NVIDIA DGX A100. YOLO-World XL achieved an overall mean Average Precision (mAP at IoU 0.50--0.95) of 0.863, but per-class performance varied widely: 94.2 percent for cracks on smooth PVC versus only 73.9 percent for cracks on textured tissue, a gap of over 20 percentage points. This gap appeared in both models, showing it is not specific to one architecture. An analysis across Intersection-over-Union (IoU) thresholds revealed that the model can detect cracks on textured surfaces (97.1 percent AP at IoU 0.50) but fails to produce precise bounding boxes, dropping 23.1 percentage points at stricter thresholds. Three data augmentation strategies (CutMix, multi-scale training, and their combination) were tested but none significantly reduced the gap (all p-values above 0.05 after Bonferroni correction). These results indicate that surface texture, rather than data quantity or model choice, is the primary factor limiting detection accuracy on challenging pipe materials.
\end{abstract}

% ==============================================================
%   I. INTRODUCTION
% ==============================================================
\section{Introduction}

Crack detection in piping systems is essential for maintaining structural integrity in industries such as oil and gas, water treatment, and manufacturing. Manual inspection is slow, subjective, and often unable to access confined areas. Mobile robots equipped with cameras can automate this task, and prior work on differential-drive platforms has addressed the navigation side of the problem, including trajectory error calibration \cite{dimalanta2022trajectory,dimalanta2023bluetooth} and odometry correction \cite{pangaliman2022odometry}. With reliable robot locomotion increasingly available, the bottleneck shifts to the vision side: deep learning-based visual inspection is a promising approach, but real-world pipes are made of different materials with different surface textures. Without a clear understanding of how these surface differences affect detection accuracy, models risk failing silently on certain pipe materials.

YOLO-World \cite{cheng2024yoloworld} builds on the YOLO \cite{redmon2016yolo} object detection framework by adding open-vocabulary capabilities through vision-language pre-training. This allows it to transfer well to new categories with limited labeled data. Because its pre-trained features should generalize across surface types, it is a good candidate for testing whether surface-related performance differences remain even when training data is balanced.

However, most existing studies only report overall accuracy on a single surface type, without checking whether different surfaces lead to different per-class results \cite{hsieh2020crack,liu2023survey,cha2017deep,li2024sewer}. While textured backgrounds are expected to degrade detection performance, the \textit{magnitude} of this effect under controlled conditions, and whether common data augmentation can mitigate it, has not been measured. Ma et al.\ \cite{li2024texmsyolo} showed that complex backgrounds degrade performance, but did not compare across different surface types in a controlled experiment.

Accordingly, this study investigates \textbf{the effect of pipe surface visual complexity on crack detection accuracy under strictly balanced training data}. The contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Evidence that surface texture creates a 20--22 percentage point (pp) gap in per-class Average Precision (AP), consistent across YOLO-World XL and YOLOv8x over five random seeds, and not caused by data imbalance.
    \item An analysis using different Intersection-over-Union (IoU) thresholds showing this is a \textit{localization} problem: the model detects paper cracks (97.1\% AP@50) but cannot produce precise bounding boxes, dropping 23.1~pp at stricter thresholds, while dummy crack drops only 3.9~pp.
    \item Evidence that three augmentation strategies fail to close the gap (Bonferroni-corrected $p > 0.05$), suggesting the problem goes beyond training data diversity.
\end{enumerate}

% ==============================================================
%   II. RELATED WORK
% ==============================================================
\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Crack Detection Methods}

Crack detection has been widely studied in civil infrastructure. Hsieh and Tsai \cite{hsieh2020crack} reviewed machine learning approaches from classical feature engineering to modern deep learning. Convolutional neural network (CNN)-based methods have largely replaced traditional edge detection \cite{liu2023survey}. Cha et al.\ \cite{cha2017deep} achieved 98\% accuracy on concrete surfaces using a sliding-window CNN. More recently, Lv et al.\ \cite{li2024sewer} deployed a lightweight YOLOv8n model on amphibious sewer robots for real-time inference. Ma et al.\ \cite{li2024texmsyolo} built a texture-aware network that improves defect detection on complex backgrounds. However, these studies report overall accuracy on a single surface type and do not examine how different surfaces affect per-class performance when data quantity is equal.

\subsection{YOLO-Based Object Detection}

The YOLO architecture \cite{redmon2016yolo} performs detection in a single pass, prioritizing speed. YOLOv8 \cite{jocher2023ultralytics} improved on this with a decoupled head, anchor-free predictions, and an enhanced feature pyramid network. YOLO-World \cite{cheng2024yoloworld} extends YOLOv8 by adding a Re-parameterizable Vision-Language Path Aggregation Network (RepVL-PAN) that connects visual features with CLIP \cite{radford2021clip} text embeddings. This allows the model to use text descriptions of categories (e.g., ``crack on PVC pipe'') to guide detection, which is useful for specialized tasks with limited training data.

\subsection{Data Augmentation for Hard Examples}

CutMix \cite{yun2019cutmix} creates new training images by cutting and pasting rectangular patches between samples, which helps the model focus on local features. Multi-scale training changes the input image resolution during training, making the model more robust to size differences. Copy-paste augmentation \cite{ghiasi2021copypaste} provides even more variety but requires segmentation masks, which are not available in our bounding-box-only dataset. We therefore test CutMix and multi-scale training as practical strategies that do not need masks.

\subsection{Transfer Learning for Industrial Inspection}

Models pre-trained on large datasets like COCO \cite{lin2014coco} and Objects365 \cite{shao2019objects365} learn features that transfer well to new tasks. While the effects of class imbalance on detection have been studied in detail \cite{japkowicz2023class,oksuz2021imbalance}, the question of whether visual differences between surfaces cause per-class performance gaps, even when data is balanced, has received little attention.

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Dataset Preparation}

Three types of crack specimens were prepared on blue-colored surfaces with different textures. We used controlled lab specimens rather than field samples so that surface texture is the only variable that changes between classes. This deliberate choice follows the logic of a controlled experiment: real field specimens would introduce confounding factors (crack age, weathering, lighting variation, pipe curvature, and soiling) that would preclude attributing performance differences specifically to substrate texture. The tissue-paper proxy is not intended to replicate an industrial pipe material; instead, it provides a repeatable textured surface whose visual complexity (rough, fibrous edges that blur crack boundaries) represents the class of challenging substrates that detection systems encounter in practice. The three categories range from simple to complex:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{Dummy cracks}: lines drawn on smooth PVC pipes, giving sharp, clear edges with no background texture.
    \item \textit{PVC pipe cracks}: real fractures on smooth PVC, with realistic crack shapes on a clean surface.
    \item \textit{Paper cracks}: cracks on blue-painted tissue rolls, where the rough, fibrous surface blurs the crack edges and reduces crack visibility.
\end{itemize}
This gradient of surface complexity enables a systematic study of how substrate texture affects both detection and localization accuracy. Fig.~\ref{fig:samples} shows annotated samples from each category.

The dataset is class-balanced at 400 labels per class (1,200 total), annotated using Roboflow \cite{roboflow2023}. Preprocessing included auto-orientation, resizing to $640 \times 640$, and contrast adjustment. The dataset was first split into training (88\%), validation (8\%), and test (4\%) partitions; augmentation ($3\times$) was then applied only to the training set and comprised flips, rotation ($\pm15\degree$), shear ($\pm10\degree$), brightness ($\pm25\%$), exposure ($\pm15\%$), blur (up to 1.5\,px), and noise (up to 1\%), yielding 2,292 training images. No augmentation was applied to the validation (217) or test (108) partitions, ensuring that evaluation images remain unseen during training. Table~\ref{tab:dataset} summarizes the composition.

\subsection{Model Architecture and Training}

YOLO-World XL (YOLOv8x-worldv2, 72.9M parameters, 273.3 giga floating-point operations (GFLOPs)) was chosen as the primary model. It combines visual features with CLIP \cite{radford2021clip} text embeddings through a RepVL-PAN module, giving it the strongest feature representations in the YOLO family. This ensures that any per-class performance differences are attributable to surface complexity rather than insufficient model capacity. The model was pre-trained on COCO \cite{lin2014coco}, Objects365 \cite{shao2019objects365}, and the GoldG grounding dataset (as described in \cite{cheng2024yoloworld}). We also included a standard YOLOv8x \cite{jocher2023ultralytics} baseline (without vision-language capabilities) to check whether the surface effect appears in a different architecture as well.

Both models were fine-tuned on an NVIDIA DGX A100-40GB for 100 epochs using the AdamW optimizer \cite{loshchilov2019adamw} with a learning rate of $2 \times 10^{-4}$, weight decay of 0.05, and $640 \times 640$ input resolution. Batch sizes were 8 for YOLO-World XL and 16 for YOLOv8x, with Ultralytics' nominal batch size ($\mathrm{nbs}=64$) automatically scaling the effective learning rate. All classes were trained together with the same settings and no class-specific adjustments, so any per-class differences can only come from the surface properties. Early stopping with a patience of 20 epochs was used, and the best model was chosen based on validation mAP@50-95. Each model was trained five times with seeds 0, 1, 2, 42, and 123, while the dataset splits stayed the same across all 10 runs. The low standard deviations across runs (std $\leq 0.014$) indicate stable training. Fig.~\ref{fig:training} confirms smooth convergence with no overfitting. Experiments were tracked using Weights \& Biases \cite{biewald2020wandb}.

\subsection{Evaluation and Mitigation Strategies}

We evaluate performance using the COCO protocol \cite{lin2014coco} with precision, recall, F1 score, and mAP. The main metric is mean Average Precision (mAP)@50-95, which averages AP across ten IoU thresholds from 0.50 to 0.95. This metric penalizes loose bounding boxes more than mAP@50, making it sensitive to localization quality.

To test whether data augmentation can reduce the surface-related AP gap, we applied three strategies to YOLO-World XL only, keeping all other settings the same: (1)~\textbf{CutMix} \cite{yun2019cutmix} with probability 0.5; (2)~\textbf{Multi-scale training} at $960 \times 960$, evaluated at $640 \times 640$; and (3)~\textbf{Combined} (both together). Each strategy was trained with the same five seeds for a fair comparison. Batch size was reduced to 4 for multi-scale and combined strategies due to memory limits at the higher resolution.

\section{Experimental Results}

\subsection{Overall and Per-Class Performance}

For the best single run (seed~42), YOLO-World XL achieves 86.9\% mAP@50-95 with 98.4\% precision, 97.7\% recall, and 98.1\% F1 score, showing strong overall performance. However, results vary substantially across crack types: dummy crack scores 95.6\%, PVC pipe crack 91.2\%, and paper crack only 74.0\%, a gap of 21.6~pp despite each class having 400 labels. This ranking matches the surface complexity order from Section~III-A: smooth surfaces score highest, textured surfaces score lowest.

Fig.~\ref{fig:qualitative} shows example detections for all three classes. The bounding boxes on dummy and PVC pipe cracks fit tightly around the crack edges, but paper crack predictions exhibit noticeably lower localization precision, particularly at crack endpoints where the edges fade into the textured background. Multi-seed averages in Section~\ref{sec:model_comparison} confirm a consistent 20--22~pp gap in both models, showing this is not a random result from one training run.

\subsection{Model Comparison: YOLO-World XL vs.\ YOLOv8x}
\label{sec:model_comparison}

We compared YOLO-World XL (${\sim}$72.9M parameters) and YOLOv8x (${\sim}$68.2M parameters) across five seeds (Table~\ref{tab:model_comparison}, Fig.~\ref{fig:model_comparison}). YOLO-World XL scores higher on all metrics. Without Bonferroni correction, three metrics reach $p < 0.05$: mAP@50 ($p=0.038$), recall ($p=0.024$), and F1 ($p=0.025$); however, none survive Bonferroni correction for five comparisons ($\alpha = 0.01$). All five metrics show large effect sizes (Cohen's $d$ 1.06--1.44), suggesting the advantage is real but the sample size ($n=5$) limits statistical power.

More importantly, the ranking of crack types is the same in both models: dummy crack scores highest, followed by PVC pipe crack, with paper crack always the lowest. The gap between the best and worst class is 20.2~pp for YOLO-World XL and 21.5~pp for YOLOv8x. Since these two models use different pre-training approaches (vision-language vs.\ vision-only), this consistency shows the performance gap comes from the surface properties, not from a specific model design. Both models exceed real-time throughput ($>$37 frames per second (FPS) on an A100 GPU at $640 \times 640$ input, batch size 1). Deployment on robot-mounted edge devices would require further benchmarking, though lighter YOLO variants have demonstrated real-time performance on embedded platforms \cite{li2024sewer}.

\subsection{Mitigation Strategy Evaluation}
\label{sec:mitigation}

None of the three strategies improved performance (Table~\ref{tab:mitigation}, Figs.~\ref{fig:mitigation_perclass} and~\ref{fig:mitigation_gap}). Compared to the baseline, CutMix lowered mAP@50-95 by 2.3~pp ($p=0.085$), Multi-Scale by 2.4~pp ($p=0.147$), and Combined by 5.8~pp ($p=0.115$). None of these changes were statistically significant after Bonferroni correction ($\alpha \approx 0.0167$). The gap between surface types was also not reduced: Multi-Scale narrowed it the most ($20.2 \to 18.1$~pp, $p=0.334$), while Combined brought it to 18.4~pp but at the cost of lower overall accuracy.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Substrate Complexity as the Governing Factor}

Previous work has shown that textured backgrounds degrade detection accuracy \cite{li2024texmsyolo,liu2023survey}, but this study quantifies the magnitude of this effect when each class contains an identical number of training labels. Because all three classes have exactly 400 labels, the 20--22~pp AP gap cannot be explained by data imbalance alone. Comparing AP@50 with AP@50-95 shows that this is mainly a \textit{localization} problem (Fig.~\ref{fig:perclass_ap}): paper crack keeps 97.1\% AP@50 but drops to 74.0\% at AP@50-95, a 23.1~pp gap, while dummy crack drops only 3.9~pp (seed~42; the same pattern holds across all five seeds in Table~\ref{tab:model_comparison}). In other words, the model detects paper cracks reliably but cannot produce tight bounding boxes when crack edges blend into the textured background. We note that labeling difficulty may also play a role: placing bounding boxes on textured tissue is harder than on smooth PVC, so part of the AP@50-95 gap may come from less consistent ground-truth labels rather than model error alone. Importantly, dummy and PVC pipe cracks are both on smooth PVC and differ by only 3.4~pp despite having different crack shapes, while the change from smooth PVC to textured tissue produces a 20--22~pp gap. This confirms that substrate texture, not crack shape, is the main factor. YOLO-World's CLIP-based pre-training gives a small advantage ($+0.83$~pp mAP@50-95), but the transferred features help most on high-contrast defects, pointing to a fundamental visual challenge.

\subsection{Limits of Augmentation-Based Mitigation}

All three strategies failed to close the gap, which suggests the problem is not simply a lack of scale or appearance variety during training. Instead, the gap is likely attributable to a more fundamental challenge: paper cracks exhibit lower contrast and poorly defined edges, making precise localization difficult regardless of the augmentation strategy employed.

Two observations support this view. First, the localization gap (AP@50 minus AP@50-95) for paper crack is much larger than for other classes across all strategies (23.1~pp vs.\ 3.9~pp for dummy), confirming that the detector identifies paper cracks but cannot localize them precisely. Second, the Combined strategy hurt \textit{all} classes rather than just improving paper crack, with PVC pipe crack showing the largest drop ($-7.3$~pp). This suggests that aggressive augmentation harms precise localization across the board. We note that Multi-Scale and Combined strategies used batch size 4 (vs.\ 8 for Baseline) due to memory limits at higher resolution; noisier gradient updates may partly explain their worse performance, though CutMix (also batch 8) still fell short of the Baseline. Closing the gap may therefore need approaches beyond training-time augmentation, such as substrate-specific fine-tuning, domain adaptation, or better labeling methods for low-contrast substrates.

\subsection{Practical Implications}

These findings have practical consequences for deploying crack detection on real pipes. First, practitioners should expect large performance differences across pipe materials and should test detection models on each target material separately rather than relying on a single overall mAP score. Second, because the gap is mainly about localization, detection-level alarms (at IoU~0.50) remain effective even on difficult surfaces, but applications requiring precise crack measurements must account for degraded bounding box accuracy on textured surfaces. Third, because standard augmentation did not close the gap, material-specific training data or domain adaptation may be needed when deploying across pipes made of different materials.

\subsection{Limitations}

Several limitations should be kept in mind:

\textit{Test set size.} The test set contains 108 images (about 36 per class). The multi-seed design accounts for training randomness, but per-class AP estimates still carry uncertainty that larger test sets would reduce. We leave bootstrapped confidence intervals to future work.

\textit{Controlled proxies vs.\ real pipes.} Controlled proxies allow isolation of the substrate texture effect, but at the cost of real-world realism. The exact size of the AP gap may differ on actual pipe failures, where factors like crack age, weathering, dirt, and uneven lighting interact with substrate properties.

\textit{Confounding factors.} Crack creation methods differ across classes (drawn lines vs.\ physical fractures vs.\ torn tissue), which partly mixes texture effects with crack shape effects. The 3.4~pp dummy--PVC gap (Section~V-A) gives an upper bound on this confound, which is small compared to the 20--22~pp substrate gap. Also, all specimens share a blue color, which limits the generalizability of these results to other pipe colors.

\textit{Annotation consistency.} The discussion notes that labeling cracks on textured surfaces is inherently more difficult. However, no inter-annotator agreement metrics (e.g., IoU between independent annotators) were collected, so the portion of the AP gap attributable to label noise versus model error remains unquantified.

\textit{Model scope.} Only two YOLO-family models were compared. The localization gap may behave differently in two-stage detectors or transformer-based architectures that employ different bounding box regression mechanisms.

%   VI. CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK
% ==============================================================
\section{Conclusion and Future Work}

This study demonstrated that substrate texture is the dominant factor governing per-class crack detection accuracy under balanced training data. Despite identical data quantities across three surface types, a consistent 20--22~pp AP gap emerged between smooth PVC and textured tissue substrates, reproducible across two architectures and five random seeds. The IoU-threshold analysis pinpointed this as a localization deficit rather than a detection failure, with practical implications: detection-level alarms remain reliable on textured surfaces, but applications requiring precise crack measurement must account for degraded bounding box accuracy. The failure of three augmentation strategies to close the gap indicates that the problem is rooted in the low edge contrast of cracks on textured substrates, not in training data diversity. Addressing this challenge will likely require approaches that operate at the feature or loss level, such as substrate-aware normalization or pixel-level segmentation objectives.

Future work should explore several directions. First, testing on real pipe failures across different materials (concrete, cast iron, clay) would show whether the substrate gap holds under real-world conditions. Second, trying different types of detectors, such as two-stage models (e.g., Faster R-CNN), transformer-based models (e.g., DETR), and segmentation models, would clarify whether the localization gap is specific to YOLO-style single-stage detectors or a more general phenomenon. Third, experiments that change only the surface texture while keeping the crack the same (e.g., identical cracks on smooth vs.\ rough surfaces) would fully separate texture effects from crack shape effects. Fourth, more targeted approaches such as domain adaptation, surface-specific feature normalization, or pixel-level segmentation losses may work better than the augmentation strategies tested here. Finally, the best-performing model can be integrated into the mobile robot platforms described in Section~I, with surface-aware confidence calibration to support reliable deployment across different pipe materials.
